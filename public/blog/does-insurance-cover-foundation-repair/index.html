Homeowner guide

Does homeowners insurance cover foundation repair? What's covered, what isn't, and what it costs

Usually not. Standard homeowners policies exclude foundation damage from settling, soil movement, poor drainage, tree roots and wear, which cause most foundation problems. They typically do cover foundation damage from a sudden, covered event such as a burst pipe, a fire, a vehicle impact or, with the right endorsement, an earthquake or sinkhole. Repair costs commonly range from a few hundred dollars for crack injection to $10,000 to $30,000 or more for piering, so most homeowners finance the work.

What homeowners insurance usually excludes

Policies are written around sudden, accidental events. Foundation problems are usually slow, so the common causes are excluded by name:

  • Settling, shifting and cracking over time.
  • Expansive or shrinking soil, the main cause of foundation movement in clay-soil regions such as Texas, Oklahoma and Colorado.
  • Poor drainage, hydrostatic pressure and groundwater seeping through basement walls.
  • Tree roots drying out soil under the slab.
  • Faulty construction or workmanship.
  • Flooding (separate flood insurance) and, without an endorsement, earthquakes and sinkholes.

Read the "exclusions" and "earth movement" sections of your policy; the wording decides your claim.

When foundation damage is covered

Coverage is more likely when a covered peril caused the damage:

  • A burst or leaking pipe under the slab that washes out soil (the water damage itself is often covered; the resulting foundation repair sometimes is, depending on policy language).
  • Fire, explosion, lightning or a vehicle hitting the house.
  • Earthquake or sinkhole if you bought that endorsement (sinkhole coverage is a specific add-on in states like Florida).
  • Collapse from hidden decay or insect damage, in some policies.

If you believe a covered event caused the damage, document it before repairs: photos, a plumber's leak report, and an independent structural engineer's assessment of cause. Insurers deny foundation claims routinely, so the cause evidence matters more than in most claims.

How to pay for foundation repair

Because insurance rarely helps, most homeowners finance the whole repair. Options:

  • Unsecured personal loan for repairs up to the lender's cap; funds in days, no lien.
  • Contractor financing offered by many foundation companies through lender partners.
  • Home equity loan or HELOC for large pier jobs, if the home has equity; note that an appraiser will see the foundation issue, so get the engineer's report and a repair plan first.
  • Government-backed programs: FHA Title I property improvement loans cover structural repairs through HUD-approved lenders, and USDA Section 504 offers repair loans and grants to eligible rural homeowners.

How to file a foundation claim that has a chance

  1. Stop the cause if it's a leak, and keep the plumber's invoice and report.
  2. Document everything: dated photos of cracks, doors that stick, sloping floors, water intrusion.
  3. Get an independent structural engineer's report on cause and scope before the adjuster visits. Insurers lean on "earth movement"; an engineer who attributes the damage to a sudden covered event is your strongest evidence.
  4. File promptly and give the adjuster the engineer's report.
  5. If denied, ask for the denial in writing citing the policy language, consider a second engineer, and know that your state's insurance department takes complaints. Public adjusters and attorneys sometimes take large claims on contingency.

Regional soils and why Texas, Oklahoma and Colorado see so many claims

Expansive clay soils swell when wet and shrink when dry, lifting and dropping slabs and footings through each cycle. Much of Texas, Oklahoma, Colorado's Front Range and parts of the Midwest sit on these soils, which is why foundation companies are so common there and why insurers write earth-movement exclusions so carefully. In those regions, drainage and consistent moisture around the foundation prevent more damage than any repair. Coastal and river areas face different problems: high water tables and hydrostatic pressure against basement walls.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Taking the foundation company's word on cause. Get an independent engineer; they don't sell piers.
  • Repairing before documenting if you intend to claim.
  • Ignoring drainage after an expensive repair; gutters, grading and a French drain protect the investment.
  • Financing without a warranty review. Transferable lifetime warranties on piers matter at resale.
  • Applying to many lenders at once in a panic; use soft-pull pre-qualification first.

Questions to ask the foundation contractor

  1. Will you work from an independent structural engineer's report, or only your own inspection?
  2. What repair method do you recommend and why, and what are the alternatives?
  3. How many piers, to what depth, and what is the price per pier if more are needed?
  4. Is the warranty transferable, and what exactly does it cover?
  5. What happens to flooring, plumbing and finishes during the lift, and who repairs them?
  6. Are permits and an engineer's final inspection included?
  7. Is the price the same for cash as for your financing plan?

Repair now or wait? Think in monthly terms

Foundation problems rarely get cheaper. A crack that costs $1,500 to inject this year can become a $15,000 pier job after two more wet-dry cycles. Financing a modest repair early is often the cheaper path: $8,000 at an illustrative 10.99% over 60 months is about $174 a month, against a much larger job later. The exception is cosmetic cracking that an engineer confirms is stable, which can be monitored rather than repaired.

What lenders look at when you finance a foundation repair

Unsecured home improvement loans are approved on the same five things whatever the project: your credit score and recent payment history, your income and how much of it already goes to debt, the amount you're asking for, how long you've had credit, and whether the application is complete. Lenders that fund foundation repairs usually see requests in the $5,000 to $40,000 range, and a request near the low end of that is approved more often and at a better rate than one near the top.

Four moves improve offers everywhere: reduce the amount (rebates, a smaller scope, a down payment), pay a card balance below about 30% of its limit before the next statement, have pay stubs or bank statements ready so the application is complete, and check options with a soft-pull pre-qualification before any hard application. If your score is on the lower side, a co-applicant with stronger credit can open better terms; they share full responsibility for the loan. Home equity products add two more checks: an appraisal or valuation and a cushion of equity the lender wants left after the loan, commonly 15% to 20% of the home's value.

Questions to ask every lender

  1. What is the APR including fees, and is the rate fixed?
  2. Is there an origination fee, and is it deducted from the loan amount?
  3. Is any promotional period deferred interest or waived interest?
  4. Is there a prepayment penalty?
  5. When is the contractor paid, and when does my first payment start?
  6. Does checking my options use a soft or hard credit inquiry?

Choosing the contractor

The quote is only as good as the company behind it. Get two or three itemized quotes from licensed, insured foundation contractors, and insist on an independent structural engineer's report before signing with any of them on the same scope, confirm permits are included, and check reviews that go back more than a season. Read any financing documents separately from the work contract: the loan is with the lender, not the contractor. The Federal Trade Commission's guide to hiring a contractor, linked in the sources below, lists the warning signs, including pressure to pay the whole job up front and steering you to a lender before you've seen a written price.

Frequently asked questions

Does insurance cover foundation cracks?

Not when the cracks come from settling, soil movement or drainage. Cracks caused by a covered event such as a burst pipe or a vehicle impact may be covered. The cause, documented by an engineer or plumber, decides it.

Does insurance cover foundation repair from a plumbing leak?

Often partly. The water damage from a sudden leak is usually covered; whether the resulting foundation repair is covered depends on the policy's wording about earth movement and the leak being sudden rather than long-term.

How much does foundation repair cost on average?

Minor crack repairs run a few hundred to a few thousand dollars; piering typically $10,000 to $30,000 or more depending on the number of piers; waterproofing $5,000 to $15,000.

Can I finance foundation repair with bad credit?

Options exist at higher APRs, and government-backed programs may help eligible homeowners. A smaller scope, a co-applicant and a soft-pull pre-qualification improve your chances.

Should I get a second foundation opinion?

Yes. Foundation companies sell repairs; an independent structural engineer's report, usually a few hundred dollars, tells you the cause and the minimum fix.

Can I sell a house with foundation problems?

Yes, with disclosure, usually at a discount. A completed repair with a transferable warranty is worth far more at sale than an unrepaired problem.
