Furnace and AC replacement financing: what to expect

Furnace and AC replacements are usually financed with an unsecured installment loan, either through the HVAC contractor's lender partner or an independent lender, over three to ten years. As an illustration, a $7,000 furnace at 11.99% APR is about $156 a month over five years, and a $14,000 full system is about $247 a month over seven. Approval and rate depend on your credit and income.

What you're actually paying for

"A new furnace" or "a new AC" can mean very different jobs. Before financing, make sure you know which one you're quoting:

  • Furnace only. Replacing a gas or electric furnace, keeping the existing outdoor AC unit.
  • AC condenser and coil. The outdoor unit and the matching indoor evaporator coil. Replacing only the outdoor unit with a mismatched coil can hurt efficiency and void warranties.
  • Full split system. Furnace or air handler, coil and outdoor unit together, often the best value if both halves are old.
  • Heat pump. Heats and cools with one outdoor unit, sometimes paired with a gas furnace as backup (a dual-fuel system).
  • Ductwork. Leaky or undersized ducts add cost but can be the difference between a system that works and one that doesn't.

What drives the price

HVAC quotes vary with system size (tonnage or BTUs), efficiency rating, brand and warranty, whether ductwork, electrical or gas line changes are needed, permits, how hard the equipment is to reach, and local labor rates. A furnace alone is often several thousand dollars; a full system replacement is commonly well into five figures. Getting two or three quotes on the same scope is the best way to understand your local price.

Ask each contractor for an itemized quote that lists the model numbers, efficiency ratings, warranty terms (parts and labor, and for how long), what's included for ducts and thermostats, and whether permits and haul-away are in the price. Model numbers let you compare like for like.

Repair or replace?

A common rule of thumb is to compare the repair cost with the age of the system: if the system is near the end of its expected life and the repair is a large fraction of a replacement, replacement often makes more sense. Other signs it's time: repeated breakdowns, an AC that uses phased-out R-22 refrigerant, a cracked heat exchanger on a furnace (a safety issue, so take it seriously), or energy bills climbing while comfort falls.

If a repair is reasonable and safe, it can be the cheapest way to get through a season and plan the replacement properly, with time to compare financing.

How furnace and AC financing works

Most HVAC financing is a fixed-rate installment loan: you borrow the cost of the job, the lender pays the contractor when the work is done, and you repay in equal monthly payments. There are three common routes.

  • Contractor lender programs. The HVAC company offers financing at the quote, often through one or two lender partners. Convenient, sometimes promotional. Ask about deferred interest and dealer fees.
  • Independent personal loans. You apply directly with a lender and pay the contractor yourself. Good for comparing against the contractor's offer.
  • Home equity. A HELOC or home equity loan can be cheaper but is slower and secured by the home. Rarely the right tool for an emergency replacement.

Some utilities also run on-bill financing programs for qualifying efficient equipment. Ask your utility whether one exists in your area.

Efficiency ratings in plain English

Quotes are full of acronyms, and they matter because higher ratings cost more up front. The ones you'll see:

  • SEER2 measures cooling efficiency for air conditioners and heat pumps under the testing standard that took effect in 2023. Higher is more efficient. Minimum required levels vary by region of the country.
  • AFUE is the share of fuel a furnace turns into heat. An 80% AFUE furnace wastes about a fifth of its fuel up the flue; 95% and higher units are condensing furnaces that need different venting.
  • HSPF2 measures heat pump heating efficiency. In colder climates, ask about cold-climate heat pumps and how well the unit performs at low outdoor temperatures.

The financing question is whether the extra cost of a higher rating pays back over the years you'll own the system. In a mild climate with modest bills, the payback on top-tier efficiency can be long. In a hot or cold climate with high usage, it can be meaningful. Ask the contractor to estimate annual operating cost for each option so you can compare total cost, not just the sticker price.

How promotional plans and dealer fees work

Many HVAC companies advertise offers like "0% for 18 months" or very low payments. They can be good deals, but understand the mechanics.

Deferred interest plans charge interest from day one in the background. If the full balance isn't paid by the end of the promotion, all of that interest is added at once. Waived interest plans genuinely charge nothing during the promotion. The wording in the agreement tells you which one you have.

Low-rate offers are often paid for by a dealer fee, a percentage of the loan the lender charges the contractor. Some contractors absorb it; others build it into the price. A fair question is: "What would the price be if I paid cash or used my own lender?" If the answer is several hundred dollars lower, compare that against the promotional offer. A $10,000 job at an illustrative 9.99% over 60 months costs about $212 a month; a "0%" offer on a price inflated to $10,800 over the same term costs $180 a month, so the math isn't always what the advertisement suggests.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. What is the APR, and what will I repay in total?
  2. Is any promotional rate deferred interest, and what happens if I don't pay the balance in time?
  3. Is there a dealer fee, and is it built into the price I'm being quoted? Would the price be lower if I paid another way?
  4. Is there a prepayment penalty?
  5. When does the lender pay the contractor, and when does my first payment start?
  6. Does the quote include permits, a load calculation and haul-away of the old equipment?
  7. Who handles warranty registration, and what does the labor warranty cover?

Timing your replacement

Emergency replacements happen in the worst weather, when contractors are busiest. If your system is old and you can plan ahead, spring and fall are often easier for scheduling, and you have time to compare quotes and financing calmly. Planning ahead also lets you check your credit, pay down card balances and gather income documents before you apply.

Even in an emergency, you usually have a little room. Many contractors can install a temporary heater or make a short-term repair to keep the house safe for a few days. Use that window to get a second quote and check one independent financing option against the contractor's plan. A day or two of comparison on a five-figure purchase is time well spent, and a good contractor will understand.

Frequently asked questions

Can you finance a furnace replacement?

Yes. Furnace replacements are commonly financed with installment loans through the HVAC contractor or an independent lender. For example, $7,000 at an illustrative 11.99% APR is about $156 a month over 60 months.

How long are furnace and AC loans?

Terms commonly run from 3 to 10 years, and some programs go longer. A longer term lowers the payment but increases total interest.

Is it cheaper to replace the furnace and AC together?

Often, yes. Replacing both at once can save on labor and ensure the indoor and outdoor components are matched. If one is fairly new, replacing only the failed half can make sense.

What credit score do I need for HVAC financing?

Each lender sets its own criteria. Stronger credit generally means more choices and lower APRs; some lenders consider lower scores at higher rates or with a co-applicant.

Should I choose a heat pump instead of a furnace and AC?

It depends on your climate, fuel costs and existing equipment. Heat pumps can heat and cool efficiently with one system, and cold-climate models work in much lower temperatures than older units. In very cold areas, a dual-fuel setup with a gas furnace backup is common. Ask for operating cost estimates for each option.

Are there rebates for new heat pumps or furnaces?

Many utilities and some states offer rebates on qualifying efficient equipment. Federal tax incentives have changed in recent years, so check current IRS guidance or ask a tax professional.
